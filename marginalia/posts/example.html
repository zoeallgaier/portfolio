Chimerism

April 18, 2026
essay identity

There is a medical condition called chimerism. It happens when two fertilized eggs fuse early in development, becoming a single organism. The resulting person carries two complete sets of DNA — two genetic identities folded into one body. Some chimeric people have discovered their condition only when their children's DNA didn't match theirs. Some have tested positive for their own twin's DNA in a paternity dispute they were supposed to win.

I think about chimerism a lot when I try to describe what I do for work. I am a graphic designer and a motion designer and a web designer. I started in video — obsessed with narrative, obsessed with the frame. Then I needed to design the whole frame, and I spent four years learning print, brand identity, logo animation, the works. Now I make websites that move the same way my films used to, and campaigns where every asset — print, digital, motion — comes from the same gesture.

The person who can only do one thing is probably just afraid to try the second thing.

The design world has a complicated relationship with people who do too many things. There's a suspicion that generalists are secretly mediocre at everything, hiding their shallow depth behind apparent breadth. I've felt that suspicion. I've internalized it at various points and tried to specialize — to pick a lane, stay in it, make the work legible to people who need a one-line answer to "so what do you do?"

The lane doesn't fit

The problem is that the lane metaphor assumes you're on a road. I'm not on a road. The work I make exists in the overlap between disciplines, in the places where graphic and motion and web stop being separate things and become one coherent idea moving through different surfaces. A campaign that lives on a billboard and a website and an event backdrop isn't three projects — it's one project that has learned to exist in three states.

Chimeric organisms don't have an identity crisis about their dual DNA. The DNA is just what they are. Both genomes coexist in different tissues, doing their jobs, making one functional person. The plurality isn't a problem to be resolved.

I've started thinking about my practice the same way. Not two things fused awkwardly together, but one thing that can express itself in multiple material forms — because the fundamental gesture, the way I think about a problem, is consistent across all of them. The inconsistency is the surface. Underneath it, there's only one approach: tell the truth as clearly and as beautifully as possible, and don't stop until every part of the frame is doing its job.

If you've got a project where graphic, motion, and web all need to show up as one thing, I'd love to hear about it.
